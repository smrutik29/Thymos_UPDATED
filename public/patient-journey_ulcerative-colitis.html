What changes long-term
🚽 Bowel habits change permanently. Loose stools, urgency, and increased frequency are common for months. 46% still take GI medication years later.
🩺 Ostomy risk: 40–44% of colectomy patients end up with a permanent ostomy — significantly affecting body image, sexual function, and daily life.
🧠 Mental health impact. Depression affects 39–43% of ostomy patients — rising from 6% pre-op to 31% by one year.12,13 Women have 2× the odds of clinical depression vs. men (aOR 2.08). Men score significantly worse on overall psychosocial adjustment and social engagement post-ostomy (p<0.001).14 Patients aged 19–45 carry 52% higher anxiety risk than those over 65. Both sexes are underscreened.
💊 Lifelong supplementation. Vitamin B12, D, A, E, K, iron, calcium, and magnesium deficiencies often require ongoing management.
📊 First-year mortality risk is 15–30% above baseline — far higher than the commonly cited 30-day figure of 1–3%.

The Other Side of the Bill

What your insurer is paying

Ulcerative colitis imposes a substantial and rising cost on commercial insurers. Biologic drugs — not the colectomy patients often fear most — are the dominant cost driver, and step therapy requirements delay rather than reduce that spend.

Biologic-treated ulcerative colitis patients cost payers substantially more than those on conventional therapy alone — commercial claims analyses report total annual healthcare costs 3–6× higher once a patient escalates to biologic therapy.16 Annual colectomy rates run 9–15% depending on prior biologic exposure, adding a $55,000–$83,000 one-time cost spike on top of ongoing drug spend.10
⚑ Why early biologic access saves payers money
Step therapy requirements mandate a thiopurine trial — and often a first anti-TNF failure — before newer mechanisms are approved, adding months of active, undertreated inflammation. That inflammation drives the loss of response that forces dose escalation and drug switching, and it accrues the colonic damage behind the 9–15% annual colectomy rate10 — costs that land on the payer regardless of which drug tier delayed adequate control.
