How Guillain-Barré syndrome is typically treated

Unlike most other autoimmune conditions, Guillain-Barré syndrome is usually a single acute event rather than a lifelong chronic disease — but the treatment choice made in the first days of admission has lasting consequences for both recovery and cost.

What the recovery period and cost actually involve
🏥 The choice of first-line treatment measurably affects both recovery and the bill. In one statewide database analysis, IVIG patients had a shorter median hospital stay (7 vs. 14 days), fewer in-hospital infections, and lower average hospitalization costs than plasmapheresis patients ($115,950 vs. $158,000).3
🌀 Adding a second treatment doesn't clearly help. A critical reassessment found no association between adding plasma exchange after IVIG and improved short-term outcomes — only an association with increased cost and longer hospitalization.7
🧠 Autonomic instability is a serious, under-discussed complication. Cardiac arrhythmias and blood pressure instability are common during the acute phase and are a major contributor to ICU-level mortality risk, independent of respiratory failure.
🔁 A small subset of patients don't have a truly monophasic course. Some patients initially diagnosed with Guillain-Barré syndrome go on to develop a relapsing or chronic course consistent with CIDP — a distinction that matters enormously for long-term treatment planning and is sometimes only clear in retrospect.
⚠ “Acute” does not mean “short” for every patient
Most Guillain-Barré syndrome patients do recover, and treatment is genuinely effective when started early. But a meaningful minority face a recovery measured in months to years, some degree of permanent residual weakness, and — rarely — an ongoing chronic course that the initial “acute illness” framing doesn't prepare anyone for.

⚑ Why the first-line choice matters more than it seems
IVIG and plasma exchange are considered equally efficacious, but real-world data show meaningfully different costs and hospital courses between them — and no added benefit at all from combining both. The treatment decision made in the first 48 hours may be the single biggest cost lever in this entire episode.
