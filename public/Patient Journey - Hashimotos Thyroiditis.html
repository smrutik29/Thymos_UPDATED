How Hashimoto's is typically treated

The drugs commonly used to treat autoimmune thyroiditis (a.k.a., Hashimoto's) are cheap, and the lab test that defines "treatment success" is simple. That's exactly the problem — treatment stops at a number, even when the patient doesn't feel better.

Why the standard approach can miss it
🧪 TSH doesn't capture tissue-level thyroid hormone status for everyone. Even with a normal TSH, differences in deiodinase enzyme activity mean some patients don't achieve adequate T3 availability at the tissue level on LT4 monotherapy alone.4
🔁 The randomized evidence actually supports these patients. In a crossover RCT, patients with residual symptoms on LT4 who switched to LT3 monotherapy for 12 weeks showed measurable quality-of-life improvement — this isn't just anecdote.4
🩺 Comorbidity gets tangled with thyroid status. LT4 users have significantly more comorbidity than people who don't need thyroid hormone, and that comorbidity burden disproportionately drags down quality of life in LT4 users specifically.6
🚪 The likeliest next stop is a referral away from endocrinology. Once TSH is normal, persistent symptoms are often reframed as depression, stress, or “nothing thyroid-related left to check” — even though thyroid autoimmunity itself is associated with persisting symptoms independent of hormone levels.
⚠ Normal TSH is a lab target, not a symptom guarantee
Hashimoto's is the cheapest disease on this site to treat by prescription cost — and one of the most common places patients are dismissed once the number on the lab report looks fine. The gap between “treated” and “well” is measured in symptoms, not in TSH.
