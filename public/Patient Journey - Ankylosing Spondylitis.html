How ankylosing spondylitis is typically treated

Most doctors and insurance plans follow step therapy — starting with NSAIDs and physical therapy before approving any biologic. The problem: every month of uncontrolled inflammation contributes to irreversible spinal fusion. Ankylosing spondylitis onset typically occurs in the 20s and 30s, meaning delayed access to effective therapy maps directly to lost career and quality of life years.

What changes with advanced ankylosing spondylitis
🦴 No biologic has yet proven it halts spinal radiographic progression. Despite dramatically reducing inflammation and symptoms, neither anti-TNF nor IL-17 inhibitors have demonstrated statistically significant slowing of new syndesmophyte formation in randomized trials at 2 years.7 This means that even well-controlled patients may be accumulating structural damage silently. Early initiation of therapy is considered the best current strategy to minimize damage, but is directly undermined by step therapy delay.
💼 Ankylosing spondylitis begins in the prime working years and devastates careers early. Mean onset is age 24. Mean forced retirement age for work-disabled ankylosing spondylitis patients is 36 years — a full 29 years before standard retirement age.9 Work disability (31%), job modification (44.6%), and sick leave (12–46 days/year) together represent a societal productivity loss that far exceeds the direct drug costs of treatment.9
🫀 Cardiovascular mortality is substantially elevated. A population-based Canadian study of 21,473 ankylosing spondylitis patients found a 35% higher cardiovascular mortality risk and 60% higher cerebrovascular mortality risk vs. age- and sex-matched controls without ankylosing spondylitis.10 Aortitis, cardiac conduction abnormalities, and accelerated atherosclerosis are all documented ankylosing spondylitis comorbidities that are rarely screened for in rheumatology practice.
🧠 Depression and anxiety are underrecognised and underscreened. A US claims-based analysis of ankylosing spondylitis patients found significantly higher rates of depression and anxiety post-diagnosis compared to matched controls.12 Depression rates in ankylosing spondylitis are 2–3× the general population, driven by chronic pain, restricted mobility, sleep disruption from night pain, and the psychosocial burden of disability at a young age.
👁️ Uveitis affects ~25–30% of ankylosing spondylitis patients over their lifetime — causing acute painful eye inflammation that can recur, may cause vision impairment, and requires prompt ophthalmology involvement. It is the most common extra-articular manifestation of ankylosing spondylitis. IBD (Crohn's or UC) occurs in ~5–10%.
⚠ Timing matters: the window before structural damage closes
Structural spinal damage in ankylosing spondylitis begins within 2–5 years of symptom onset. The 6–8 year diagnostic delay means that many patients have already accumulated irreversible fusion by the time they receive a diagnosis, let alone a biologic. Step therapy requirements that mandate NSAID failure before biologic access add further months of uncontrolled inflammation at the exact window where intervention would have the greatest impact.

⚑ Why early biologic access saves payers money
Step therapy requirements mandate NSAID failure before biologics — adding months of uncontrolled inflammation during the structural damage window. Ankylosing spondylitis work disability (31%) and early forced retirement (mean age 36) impose indirect societal costs that can exceed $1M per patient in lost lifetime earnings. The 6–8 year diagnostic delay alone contributes substantially to downstream medical costs through accumulated joint damage, cardiovascular comorbidity, spinal fracture, and surgical intervention. Indirect costs (productivity loss, disability, caregiver burden) account for the majority of the total economic burden of ankylosing spondylitis — a figure that is never on an insurer's remittance advice.
