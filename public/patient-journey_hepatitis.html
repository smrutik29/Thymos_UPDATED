What long-term management actually looks like
🔁 Most patients never truly “stop” treatment. Because periodic liver biopsy findings don't reliably predict who will relapse, and because relapse rates after withdrawal run as high as 80%, most patients end up on some form of indefinite immunosuppression rather than a defined treatment course.9
💊 The steroid-sparing strategy exists precisely because long-term steroids are so costly to the body. The entire rationale for adding azathioprine is to allow prednisone tapering — an implicit acknowledgment that years of high-dose steroids carry real cumulative harm (bone loss, diabetes, cataracts, weight gain).
🫀 Transplant is not a rare outcome — it's a real minority endpoint. Between 10% and 20% of autoimmune hepatitis patients eventually require liver transplantation, most often after progression to decompensated cirrhosis despite treatment.4
🔄 Transplantation doesn't end the disease. autoimmune hepatitis recurs in the new liver in 36–68% of transplant recipients within 5 years — meaning even the most aggressive intervention available doesn't guarantee a cure.8
⚠ Remission and cure are not the same thing
Autoimmune hepatitis treatment is genuinely effective at inducing remission for most patients — that's real, documented success. But “in remission” in autoimmune hepatitis almost always means “on ongoing immunosuppression,” and the disease is quick to return the moment that support is removed.

⚑ Why autoimmune hepatitis is cheap on paper but expensive in practice
The drugs themselves are some of the least expensive on this site. The real cost driver is the disease's biology: high relapse rates after withdrawal mean many patients cycle back to induction-dose steroids repeatedly, and 10–20% eventually reach transplant — an outcome that dwarfs years of prior drug spend combined.
